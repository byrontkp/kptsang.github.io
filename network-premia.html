change risk premia?

Compare sourcing arrangements and follow their effects on risk compensation. The two-period model explains the basic channels; the infinite-horizon model adds ongoing investment and persistent productivity shocks.

INFINITE HORIZON · 14 SECTORS · FIXED STEADY SECTOR SIZES

Do network effects survive investment and persistent shocks?

They do in this model, even when every sector’s steady Domar weight is held fixed. The observed sourcing arrangement lowers the aggregate premium at the measured persistence estimates. Across a wider range of shocks and preferences, the sign can reverse.

The aggregate is a portfolio of sector capital, weighted by steady capital value. “Network effect” means the difference between its premia under the two sourcing arrangements. One basis point (bp) = 0.01 percentage points. These are productive-capital returns, not levered equity returns.

What exactly is held fixed?
ℓj = pjyj/Y,   Qjk = ηjℓjWjk

ℓ is the vector of steady Domar weights: gross sector revenue divided by model GDP Y, which includes consumption and investment. η is the intermediate-input share. W records intermediate expenditure shares, with buyers in rows and suppliers in columns. Q records the resulting input flows as shares of GDP.

We hold every row total and column total of Q fixed. The benchmark sets each diagonal entry to the smaller of its row and column totals, then matches the remaining purchases and sales proportionally. This preserves every steady Domar weight, sector value-added share and capital-value portfolio weight. It does not fix physical quantities, revenues in every shock realization, or stochastic mean sizes. The matched sizes are the model’s baseline sizes, not an exact match to all observed BEA revenue shares.

When σ changes, primitive CES sourcing weights are recalibrated to preserve the target steady expenditure flows. Holding their numerical values fixed instead would generally alter expenditure shares and sector sizes. Factor shares, consumption weights and reference TFP levels remain fixed.

WHAT THE FULL EXERCISE ESTABLISHES

Network effects survive; a universal sign does not

At measured persistence, the observed network lowers the aggregate premium in all 32 combinations of input elasticity, EIS and risk aversion examined. The reductions range from 0.140 to 0.866 annual basis points. The negative effect also survives eight alternative constructions of the benchmark, tested at baseline preferences and the four input elasticities.

Across the wider grid of 416 distinct comparisons, aggregate effects range from −1.461 to +0.526 annual basis points. Professional/business services is the only sector with a positive effect throughout that grid, although its effect can be as small as 0.009 basis points. Other sectors change sign or reach near-zero effects. Sector spreads can widen or narrow. These are numerical findings for the supplied inputs and specified comparisons, not general propositions about particular industries.

At σ = 1, expenditure shares on intermediate suppliers are constant, yet the baseline aggregate effect remains −0.548 basis points. Investment and equilibrium prices can change capital-return exposure even without changing those shares. At baseline, about 97.5% of the aggregate effect is assigned to changes in return exposure in the symmetric decomposition.

The absolute premia remain small. This exercise studies mechanisms within the maintained model; it does not remedy its poor fit to macro and equity-return moments or identify an empirical network risk premium. Ranges across scenarios are not confidence intervals.

Equations, inputs and limits

How is a quarterly premium calculated and annualized?
Mt+1 = β(Ct+1/Ct)−1/ψ(Vt+1/μt)1/ψ−γRkj,t+1 = [αjpj,t+1yj,t+1/Kj,t+1 + (1 − δ)qj,t+1] / qj,tRPj,q ≡ −Covt(log Mt+1, log Rkj,t+1)Annualized premium (bp) = 4 × 10,000 × RPj,q

M is the stochastic discount factor, C consumption, V continuation utility, and μ its conditional Epstein–Zin certainty equivalent. γ is risk aversion; ψ is the EIS; β discounts future utility. α is the capital share, p the sector goods price in consumption units, y gross output, K next period’s productive capital, q its purchase price, and δ depreciation. The return includes next period’s capital income and the resale value of undepreciated capital.

Policies are first-order approximations around the deterministic steady state. Premia are leading second-order conditional covariances of log innovations. Multiplying by four expresses the quarterly covariance premium at an annual rate; it is not an exact four-quarter holding-period return. A leading arithmetic excess return also includes the risk-free-rate factor (here approximately RP/β per quarter). Higher-order mean shifts and state-dependent premia are not solved.

The two-period tab reports percentages per unspecified model period and prices a terminal residual-profit claim. Its figures are not directly comparable to these annualized returns on continuing capital.

What adjustment costs and production structure are retained?
gjt = Kj,t+1/Kjt,   Ijt = Kj,t+1 − (1 − δ)Kjtqjt = pjtgjtχ,   investment goods used = gjtχIjt

This is the retained Kekre–Lenel external-cost convention. Capital producers take sector-wide growth g as given privately, while aggregate goods absorption includes its cost. No internal adjustment-cost function is substituted. Labor supply is fixed at one and allocated across sectors. Production combines capital, labor and a CES bundle of intermediate inputs; consumption is Cobb–Douglas. No leverage, habits, disaster risk or additional shock process is introduced.

Shock equations, parameter values and variance conventions
log zjt = log z̄j + at + ujtat+1 = ρaat + saεa,t+1,   uj,t+1 = ρjujt + sjεj,t+1

Innovations ε have zero conditional means, unit variances and are mutually independent in the maintained calibration. The common factor a creates comovement in sector TFP. A full probability distribution is not imposed. Here ρ means persistence, unlike the contemporaneous correlation control in the two-period tab. σ without a subscript is input substitution; s denotes shock standard deviation.

snew = sbase √[(1 − ρnew²)/(1 − ρbase²)]

This adjustment holds each factor’s long-run variance fixed. Under fixed innovation variances, s instead stays at its baseline value and long-run uncertainty changes with ρ. Both conventions coincide at measured persistence.

Numerical checks and the scope of the scenario grid

The primary grid contains 416 distinct paired comparisons. There are 448 selectable combinations because the two variance conventions give identical measured-persistence results. An additional 40 comparisons vary the measured half-lives or common versus sector persistence; eight alternative benchmark matrices provide 32 further comparisons. Those additional checks are summarized above rather than exposed as arbitrary slider choices.

The main and supplementary runs, including intermediate network paths, contain 1,068 unique solved equilibria with no failures. The maximum error in the steady Domar restriction is below 3 × 10−16; the maximum linear Euler coefficient residual is 9.1 × 10−12. All transition roots are stable. Eighteen derivative-step checks change premia by at most approximately 0.0000011 annual basis points. These checks establish numerical consistency of the local solution, not global approximation accuracy.

Scenario data are taken from the verified 30 October 2026 robustness exercise. Saved results include the selected parameters, source-file hashes, units, sector weights and numerical-check summary. Signs in the robustness counts use a tolerance of 0.001 annual basis points; this is not a threshold for economic significance.

READING THE NETWORK CONTROLS

What does τ change?

τ (tau) measures how far the sourcing arrangement moves toward your target: 0 means own-sector inputs; 1 means the target network. At 0.5, the cross-sector weights or shares being varied are halfway to their targets. Each value describes a separate economy; τ is not time or shock persistence.

How is the target chosen when Domar weights are matched?
θ1W12 = θ2W21
W*12 = s min(1, θ2/θ1)W*21 = s min(1, θ1/θ2)

θ1 = θ and θ2 = 1 − θ are consumption expenditure shares. The intensity s selects the target: s = 1 gives the largest cross-sector purchases consistent with the match. The program solves for technology weights that deliver these purchase shares at equilibrium prices. After a productivity shock, technology weights stay fixed and purchase shares can change.

What does “second order” mean here?

The household’s expected discounted payoffs are expanded through shock variances and covariances. We solve those approximated Euler equations jointly with the exact resource accounts. No Gaussian distribution is imposed. This is a local approximation, not an exact solution for arbitrarily large shocks.

The main charts evaluate the local covariance premium at the risk-adjusted capital solution. A strictly leading-order premium instead evaluates it at the zero-risk investment equilibrium. Their difference consists of higher-order terms, so it should not be interpreted as additional second-order precision.

RPj is the covariance premium; M is the household’s stochastic discount factor, Rj is sector j’s gross return, C1 is terminal consumption, and γ is risk aversion. The consumption beta bjC measures return exposure to consumption risk. It is different from the preference parameter β and is undefined when consumption has zero variance. What this demonstration establishes

Production links can change the amount of consumption risk and each sector’s exposure to it. With endogenous investment, they also change the capital allocation before the shocks occur. The sign and the sector ranking depend on technology, final demand and the distribution of productivity shocks. These are local, illustrative model calculations, not estimates of US premia.

What does keeping Domar weights fixed mean?

A terminal Domar weight is gross sector revenue divided by the value of final consumption: ℓ = (I − ηW′)−1θ. Because gross revenue includes intermediate transactions, Domar weights need not sum to one. With the common intermediate exponent here, each sector’s share of total gross revenue is (1 − η)ℓj.

The total-effect comparison holds preferences, demand, technology exponents and shock moments fixed while changing primitive sourcing weights. Equilibrium sector sizes can change. The matched comparison instead chooses sourcing technologies that preserve the own-sector reference Domar weights θ/(1 − η). Demand and shock parameters are not recalibrated to force the match.

For two sectors, the restriction is θ1W12 = θ2W21. One cross-sector intensity therefore determines both actual expenditure shares. At intensity s and progress τ, W12 = τs min(1, θ2/θ1) and W21 = τs min(1, θ1/θ2). The program finds the primitive CES weights consistent with these shares and equilibrium prices. With endogenous investment, it solves the matching condition jointly with the initial equilibrium.

Matching holds at the terminal reference equilibrium used in the main charts. It does not fix absolute revenues, market capitalizations, initial-period sector sizes or revenues in every shock realization. After a shock, technology weights stay fixed and actual expenditure shares respond to prices. These different responses are precisely what can generate different premia.

At matched Domar weights and unchanged shock moments, the local consumption-risk component and the reference revenue-weighted average premium are identical across the two economies. Any main-chart premium difference comes from relative return exposure. This is a test of differences across sectors, not a test of whether links raise average compensation for aggregate risk. Matching removes an economically meaningful part of the total network effect; neither comparison is universally better. The chosen balanced sourcing path is a different counterfactual from the unrestricted path; it is not a unique decomposition of that original experiment.

The match is imposed at risk-adjusted capital when investment is endogenous. The separate strictly leading-order check keeps the same primitive technology and evaluates zero-risk capital; its Domar weights can differ by higher-order terms. Changing initial conditions or investment preferences may change the required technology weights while leaving the main-chart terminal exposures fixed by the selected expenditure shares.

Equations and assumptions

There are two sectors. Terminal production is yj = zjkjαMjη, with α + η < 1. The intermediate composite is CES with elasticity σ. Household consumption is Cobb–Douglas with expenditure shares θ and 1 − θ. Capital is predetermined when the terminal shocks arrive; there is no terminal labor or investment, and the terminal residual payoff is (1 − η)pjyj.

In the total-effect mode, the primitive sourcing matrix is Ω(τ) = (1 − τ)I + τΩ*. The target off-diagonal weights are ω12 and ω21. Actual shares satisfy Wjk = ωjkpk1−σ / Σlωjlpl1−σ. Each comparison recomputes goods prices, expenditure shares and the terminal allocation.

Write L = I − ηW, A = L−1, ℓ = A′θ, ν = (1 − η)ℓ and S = diag(ν). The corrected response uses D = diag(W′ν) − W′SW, H = η(1 − σ)S−1A′D and B = L − (1 − η)H. Consumption responds with ℓ′, log returns with F = 1ℓ′ − HB−1, and log goods prices with G = −B−1 + 1ℓ′. Here ν denotes gross revenue shares, distinct from depreciation δ. Revenue-share feedback is retained.

The covariance premium is RP = γFΣℓ. In percentage units, the page reports 100 × RP. It is the local approximation to E[log R] − log Rf + ½Var(log R), rather than an exact finite-risk arithmetic excess return. There is no annualization. Large shock scales can make a local approximation less informative.

Productivity is log zj = log z̄j + a + εj, where z̄ is the reference productivity setting. The common component a is independent of ε; Corr(ε1, ε2) is adjustable. Thus Σ11 = σa² + σ1², Σ22 = σa² + σ2² and Σ12 = σa² + ρσ1σ2. All covariances refer to the same period.

Demand shares remain strictly positive so both sectors are active in the own-sector benchmark. The common capital exponent, intermediate exponent and substitution elasticity follow the existing analytical model. Their sector-specific generalization would require a new derivation.

Initial-period equations and the approximation

Date-0 production uses inherited capital, labor and intermediates, with labor exponent ξ = 1 − α − η and aggregate labor fixed at one. The date-0 productivity level is one. Investment is Ij = Kj − (1 − δ)Kj,old. Under the retained Kekre–Lenel external-cost convention, qj = pj0(Kj/Kj,old)χ and investment absorbs (Kj/Kj,old)χIj units of good j. Capital producers take sector-wide growth as given. Disinvestment is permitted, as in the maintained convention.

The terminal claim pays πj = (1 − η)pj1yj1/Kj, preserving the slides’ residual-profit payoff. The SDF is M = [β/(1 − β)]C01/ψμγ−1/ψC1−γ, where μ is the Epstein–Zin certainty equivalent. The system imposes qj ≈ E[Mπj] using a second-order expansion of log expectations. This is a decentralized equilibrium calculation, not a planner problem that internalizes the investment externality.

Let c = log C1 and dj = log πj. At the reference terminal equilibrium, their gradients are ℓ and fj, and their Hessians are Qc and Qj. The certainty-equivalent correction is u = ½ tr(QcΣ) + ½(1 − γ)ℓ′Σℓ. The log-pricing correction is aj = (γ − 1/ψ)u + ½ tr[(Qj − γQc)Σ] + ½(fj − γℓ)′Σ(fj − γℓ). The solver uses log qj = log[β/(1 − β)] + (log C0 − c̄)/ψ + d̄j + aj.

Patience and the EIS now affect saving and investment. They can change network premia through the resulting capital allocation and terminal expenditure shares. The fixed-capital mode intentionally has no such channel. Shock persistence remains absent because there is only one uncertain terminal date; the controls specify its conditional covariance directly.

The deterministic and stochastic reference equilibria are numerical solutions, not a general uniqueness theorem. Small residuals establish the equations of the local approximation, not its accuracy for arbitrarily large shocks or unspecified higher moments.
