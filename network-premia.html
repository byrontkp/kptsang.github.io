READING THE NETWORK CONTROLS

What does τ change?

τ (tau) measures how far the sourcing arrangement moves toward your target: 0 means own-sector inputs; 1 means the target network. At 0.5, the cross-sector weights or shares being varied are halfway to their targets. Each value describes a separate economy; τ is not time or shock persistence.

How is the target chosen when Domar weights are matched?
θ1W12 = θ2W21
W*12 = s min(1, θ2/θ1)W*21 = s min(1, θ1/θ2)

θ1 = θ and θ2 = 1 − θ are consumption expenditure shares. The intensity s selects the target: s = 1 gives the largest cross-sector purchases consistent with the match. The program solves for technology weights that deliver these purchase shares at equilibrium prices. After a productivity shock, technology weights stay fixed and purchase shares can change.

What does “second order” mean here?

The household’s expected discounted payoffs are expanded through shock variances and covariances. We solve those approximated Euler equations jointly with the exact resource accounts. No Gaussian distribution is imposed. This is a local approximation, not an exact solution for arbitrarily large shocks.

The main charts evaluate the local covariance premium at the risk-adjusted capital solution. A strictly leading-order premium instead evaluates it at the zero-risk investment equilibrium. Their difference consists of higher-order terms, so it should not be interpreted as additional second-order precision.

RPj is the covariance premium; M is the household’s stochastic discount factor, Rj is sector j’s gross return, C1 is terminal consumption, and γ is risk aversion. Cov measures comovement across possible shocks; Var measures risk. A return that falls when consumption is low requires a positive premium.

bjC = Cov(log Rj, log C1) / Var(log C1)

The consumption beta bjC measures return exposure to consumption risk. It is different from the preference parameter β and is undefined when consumption has zero variance. The page evaluates these covariances using local responses to productivity shocks and reports 100 × RPj, in percent per period. These are approximate covariance premia, not exact arithmetic excess returns.

What this demonstration establishes

Production links can change the amount of consumption risk and each sector’s exposure to it. With endogenous investment, they also change the capital allocation before the shocks occur. The sign and the sector ranking depend on technology, final demand and the distribution of productivity shocks. These are local, illustrative model calculations, not estimates of US premia.

What does keeping Domar weights fixed mean?

A terminal Domar weight is gross sector revenue divided by the value of final consumption: ℓ = (I − ηW′)−1θ. Because gross revenue includes intermediate transactions, Domar weights need not sum to one. With the common intermediate exponent here, each sector’s share of total gross revenue is (1 − η)ℓj.

The total-effect comparison holds preferences, demand, technology exponents and shock moments fixed while changing primitive sourcing weights. Equilibrium sector sizes can change. The matched comparison instead chooses sourcing technologies that preserve the own-sector reference Domar weights θ/(1 − η). Demand and shock parameters are not recalibrated to force the match.

For two sectors, the restriction is θ1W12 = θ2W21. One cross-sector intensity therefore determines both actual expenditure shares. At intensity s and progress τ, W12 = τs min(1, θ2/θ1) and W21 = τs min(1, θ1/θ2). The program finds the primitive CES weights consistent with these shares and equilibrium prices. With endogenous investment, it solves the matching condition jointly with the initial equilibrium.

Matching holds at the terminal reference equilibrium used in the main charts. It does not fix absolute revenues, market capitalizations, initial-period sector sizes or revenues in every shock realization. After a shock, technology weights stay fixed and actual expenditure shares respond to prices. These different responses are precisely what can generate different premia.

At matched Domar weights and unchanged shock moments, the local consumption-risk component and the reference revenue-weighted average premium are identical across the two economies. Any main-chart premium difference comes from relative return exposure. This is a test of differences across sectors, not a test of whether links raise average compensation for aggregate risk. Matching removes an economically meaningful part of the total network effect; neither comparison is universally better. The chosen balanced sourcing path is a different counterfactual from the unrestricted path; it is not a unique decomposition of that original experiment.

The match is imposed at risk-adjusted capital when investment is endogenous. The separate strictly leading-order check keeps the same primitive technology and evaluates zero-risk capital; its Domar weights can differ by higher-order terms. Changing initial conditions or investment preferences may change the required technology weights while leaving the main-chart terminal exposures fixed by the selected expenditure shares.

Equations and assumptions

There are two sectors. Terminal production is yj = zjkjαMjη, with α + η < 1. The intermediate composite is CES with elasticity σ. Household consumption is Cobb–Douglas with expenditure shares θ and 1 − θ. Capital is predetermined when the terminal shocks arrive; there is no terminal labor or investment, and the terminal residual payoff is (1 − η)pjyj.

In the total-effect mode, the primitive sourcing matrix is Ω(τ) = (1 − τ)I + τΩ*. The target off-diagonal weights are ω12 and ω21. Actual shares satisfy Wjk = ωjkpk1−σ / Σlωjlpl1−σ. Each comparison recomputes goods prices, expenditure shares and the terminal allocation.

Write L = I − ηW, A = L−1, ℓ = A′θ, ν = (1 − η)ℓ and S = diag(ν). The corrected response uses D = diag(W′ν) − W′SW, H = η(1 − σ)S−1A′D and B = L − (1 − η)H. Consumption responds with ℓ′, log returns with F = 1ℓ′ − HB−1, and log goods prices with G = −B−1 + 1ℓ′. Here ν denotes gross revenue shares, distinct from depreciation δ. Revenue-share feedback is retained.

The covariance premium is RP = γFΣℓ. In percentage units, the page reports 100 × RP. It is the local approximation to E[log R] − log Rf + ½Var(log R), rather than an exact finite-risk arithmetic excess return. There is no annualization. Large shock scales can make a local approximation less informative.

Productivity is log zj = log z̄j + a + εj, where z̄ is the reference productivity setting. The common component a is independent of ε; Corr(ε1, ε2) is adjustable. Thus Σ11 = σa² + σ1², Σ22 = σa² + σ2² and Σ12 = σa² + ρσ1σ2. All covariances refer to the same period.

Demand shares remain strictly positive so both sectors are active in the own-sector benchmark. The common capital exponent, intermediate exponent and substitution elasticity follow the existing analytical model. Their sector-specific generalization would require a new derivation.

Initial-period equations and the approximation

Date-0 production uses inherited capital, labor and intermediates, with labor exponent ξ = 1 − α − η and aggregate labor fixed at one. The date-0 productivity level is one. Investment is Ij = Kj − (1 − δ)Kj,old. Under the retained Kekre–Lenel external-cost convention, qj = pj0(Kj/Kj,old)χ and investment absorbs (Kj/Kj,old)χIj units of good j. Capital producers take sector-wide growth as given. Disinvestment is permitted, as in the maintained convention.

The terminal claim pays πj = (1 − η)pj1yj1/Kj, preserving the slides’ residual-profit payoff. The SDF is M = [β/(1 − β)]C01/ψμγ−1/ψC1−γ, where μ is the Epstein–Zin certainty equivalent. The system imposes qj ≈ E[Mπj] using a second-order expansion of log expectations. This is a decentralized equilibrium calculation, not a planner problem that internalizes the investment externality.

Let c = log C1 and dj = log πj. At the reference terminal equilibrium, their gradients are ℓ and fj, and their Hessians are Qc and Qj. The certainty-equivalent correction is u = ½ tr(QcΣ) + ½(1 − γ)ℓ′Σℓ. The log-pricing correction is aj = (γ − 1/ψ)u + ½ tr[(Qj − γQc)Σ] + ½(fj − γℓ)′Σ(fj − γℓ). The solver uses log qj = log[β/(1 − β)] + (log C0 − c̄)/ψ + d̄j + aj.

Patience and the EIS now affect saving and investment. They can change network premia through the resulting capital allocation and terminal expenditure shares. The fixed-capital mode intentionally has no such channel. Shock persistence remains absent because there is only one uncertain terminal date; the controls specify its conditional covariance directly.

The deterministic and stochastic reference equilibria are numerical solutions, not a general uniqueness theorem. Small residuals establish the equations of the local approximation, not its accuracy for arbitrarily large shocks or unspecified higher moments.
